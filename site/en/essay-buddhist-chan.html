Chan Buddhism in the Journey to the West

ESSAY · E11 · CULTURE
A Buddhist plot, a Chan mind: how the novel's deepest grammar is the sudden-vs-gradual enlightenment of Chinese Zen.
A curated English summary of the project's Chinese essay Xīyóu yǔ Fójiào Chánzōng Zhuān tí (A5 · W224). Dates, the four theorists, and doctrinal claims are taken from that source; no figures are invented. This is a summary translation, not a full rendering.

Introduction: A Chan Reading ↔ the novel as mind-cultivation

The Journey to the West was written in the mid-to-late Ming (c. 1550s–1590s), exactly when Chan (Zen) Buddhism was reviving under the "Four Great Monks" — Yunqi Zhuhong, Zibo Zhenke, Hanshan Deqing, and Ouyi Zhixu. Its surface is a scripture-fetching adventure; its depth is mind-cultivation (心性修行). The heroes' very names — "mind-monkey" xinyuan (心猿), "returning the mind-monkey to the right" (心猿归正), "polishing the nature" (磨性) — are lifted straight from the Chan lexicon. Sun Wukong's arc — Great Sage (deluded mind) → mind-monkey subdued → Victorious Fighting Buddha (enlightened mind) — is the complete mythologised narrative of Chan's "seeing one's own nature and becoming a Buddha" (明心见性).

The Eight-Consciousness Cast ↔ an embodied Yogācāra

The project's essay reads the five pilgrims as an embodied map of the Yogācāra eight consciousnesses (万法唯识): Tang Sanzang is the eighth, the storehouse consciousness (阿赖耶识, the ground of mind); Wukong the sixth, discursive consciousness (意识, the operating mind); Bajie the first five, sense-consciousness (食欲/色欲); and Sha Wujing the seventh, manas (末那识, the ego-knot of self-attachment). The pilgrimage is thus not only a journey west but a progressive purification of the eight consciousnesses — "turning consciousness into wisdom" (转识成智).

Four Core Concepts ↔ mapped onto the plot

  • Seeing one's nature (明心见性). The whole arc is three movements — delusions blazing (Great Sage), subduing the deluded mind (band + trials), manifesting the true nature (Fighting Buddha). The abstract Chan "mind-nature" gains a body.
  • Sudden vs. gradual (顿悟渐修). The eighty-one trials are gradual cultivation; enlightenment at the end is sudden. Ming Chan's reconciliation of the two (顿渐不二) is exactly the novel's structure: long practice, instant awakening.
  • No-thought as the root (无念为宗). The tightening-band is "thought" made flesh — every recitation is a thought arising; its final disappearance is "no-thought" attained.
  • Precept-samadhi-wisdom (戒定慧三学). The band is precept (戒) materialised, meditation (定) is the furnace stillness, wisdom (慧) is the fiery-eye insight — the threefold training enacted.

Conclusion: A Classical Chinese Chan Text ↔ why it matters

Read this way, the novel is less a scripture-fetching tale than a Chan allegory of the mind: surface Buddhism, deep Chan. Its "取经即取心" (to fetch the scriptures is to fetch the mind) turns eighty-one ordeals into eighty-one delusions successively broken, and the cast into a living anatomy of consciousness. That is why the project treats the allegorical layer as primary — and why E10 (Quanzhen Daoism) and this essay together reveal a book wearing a Buddhist plot over a Daoist-Chan grammar.
