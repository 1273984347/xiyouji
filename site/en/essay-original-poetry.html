The Novel's Own Verse — Eight Hundred Poems in a Hundred Chapters

ESSAY 37 · SUMMARY TRANSLATION
An umbrella overview of the ~800 poems: four functions, thematic groupings, the hundred chapter-couplets, genre distribution, and a character-praise comparison.

Eight Hundred Poems in a Hundred Chapters ↔ overview

The novel carries some 800-plus poems (counts run higher by some tallies). They do four jobs: preface verse (opening a chapter, naming its theme), descriptive verse (portraying a person, scene, or fight), closing verse (summing, punctuating, leaving space), and character-composed verse (a figure declaiming). This essay is the umbrella overview that accompanies E6 (scenery poems), E31 (the opening verse), and E33 (the imagery lineage).

By Theme ↔ cosmos · praise · landscape · battle · couplet

Cosmos & opening (ch. 1): the seven-character regulated “Before chaos parted…” opens with the universe and lands on “the Tale of Liberation that is the Journey to the West.” Note “the work of Creation's cyclic aeon” — one yuán = 129,600 years — and the self-referential wink (the poem is the book's own opener yet points you to read the “Liberation Tale”). Character praise: Wukong's paean rhymes on “-ang” (bright/resounding) and turns on the word “proud” — not one “monkey” in it, yet all monkey; Tang Monk's parallel-prose praise is still where Wukong's is motion. Landscape: the Water-Curtain Cave verse writes the “misty haze” that turns a wet cave into a fairy realm — the book never paints wet, always immortal. Battle: the Havoc verse is built on parallelism plus intertextuality, a stanza per combatant, inherited from Yuan drama's face-off songs. Closing: the ch. 1 close alternates verse and plain speech — the signature of the chapter-cycled novel.

What the Poems Do ↔ narrative · commentary · ornament

They narrate (battle and journey in verse bear part of the plot — a vernacular-novel tradition of verse-prose alternation), they comment (the author's plain “the poem says: good and evil are repaid in the end” is a direct authorial voice), and they ornament (some exist only to deck a scene — the Ming novel's “all genres aboard”).
