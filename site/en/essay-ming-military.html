The Heavenly Army Was a Wei-so Regiment

ESSAY E26 · SUMMARY TRANSLATION
A hundred thousand heavenly troops who cannot catch one monkey — the Ming military's institutional collapse, replayed in heaven.
A summary translation of 明代军事制度对照专题.md. The Chinese original reads the heavenly armed forces as a precise literary replica of the decaying Ming military — triangulated through Huang Renyu, Mao Haijian, Liang Fangzhong, and Meng Sen.

The Heavenly Army Was a Wei-so Regiment ↔ the military mirror

The heavenly troops are not myth; they are the literary replica of the Ming military. The hundred-thousand-strong encirclement of Flower-Fruit Mountain mirrors the guard-battalion (卫所) paradox of "soldiers on the register but absent in camp"; Li Jing's field command mirrors the temporary supreme-commander (总兵) system of "generals who know not their troops"; Nezha as vanguard is in truth Li Jing's private soldier, mirroring the rise of the household-retainer (家丁) system; Erlang "obeys dispatches but not summonses," mirroring the semi-independent frontier tusi (土变) warlords. Written as the Ming military collapsed — guard-battalions dissolved, military farms seized, retainers replacing regulars, tusi flickering between revolt and loyalty.

Four Scholars ↔ the critical scaffold

  • Huang Renyu (1587, A Year of No Significance): the decay was institutional, not a matter of poor generals — guard-household desertion, officer-seized farms, phantom musters. Qi Jiguang's success came only by abandoning guard troops and privately recruiting the "Qi army." The "lack of mathematical management" left the ministry blind to real strength.
  • Mao Haijian (The Fall of the Heavenly Dynasty): the Opium War rout traced back to Ming military institutions — hereditary troops lost fighting spirit, civilian-controlled command emasculated generals, the artisan-register froze firearm innovation for centuries.
  • Liang Fangzhong (The Ming Granary-Chief System): the military farm (军宝) was designed to feed itself but was seized by officers; when self-supply failed, army grain was shifted onto the people, triggering desertion and hollow regiments.
  • Meng Sen (Lectures on Ming History): the ordnance evolved guard-system → recruited army → household retainers — each a patch after the last failed, each spawning new problems; the irreversible "privatisation of the army" ended in warlordism.

Heavenly Troops vs. Guard-Battalions ↔ big muster, low bite

Chapter 5 is the fullest record of a heavenly campaign. Li Jing musters thirty-six heavenly generals, seventy-two deities, a hundred thousand troops, and eighteen nets — an imposing muster. Yet the Nine Luminary Stars "drag their weapons and flee," Hu'an the messenger is "arm-sore and leg-numb," and Li Jing "orders retreat." A hundred thousand troops, eighteen nets, one monkey — repeated defeat. This is Huang's "raised a million, yet not one soldier to use": the roster and the battlefield have split apart. The Tumu crisis (1449) and the Jiajing wokou raids — dozens of pirates marching from Zhejiang to the gates of Nanjing — are the same institutional collapse.

Li Jing's Command vs. the Supreme-Commander System ↔ ad-hoc field marshal

Li Jing is a temporary commander: the Emperor names him "Demon-Subduing Grand Marshal" only when war breaks out, and he stands down when it ends — no standing "Board of War," no fixed troops. This mirrors the Ming supreme-commander (总兵) system designed to prevent military usurpation: a temporary imperial commission, troops scraped together from various guard units, producing "generals who know not their troops, troops who know not their generals." At Tumu the improvised half-million under the Zhengtong emperor was mutually uncommanded and routed by Yesen.

Nezha the Vanguard vs. the Retainer System ↔ private arms rise

Nezha fights not as a "heavenly general" but as "Li Jing's son" — his real power is kinship, not rank. This is the retainer (家丁) system made precise: commanders privately recruited, fed, and trained elite soldiers whose loyalty ran to the man, not the state. Nezha's weapons are self-cultivated, his loyalty "filial," not "official" — exactly the Ming retainer who supplied his own arms. When the regular army fails, the commander's private soldiers become the real striking power, and the army's ownership slides from state to private hand.

Erlang "Obeys Dispatch, Not Summons" vs. the Tusi System ↔ semi-independent frontier

Erlang lives at Guanjiangkou, keeps his own "Six Brothers of Meishan" and twelve hundred "Grass-Head Soldiers," and answers the Jade Emperor's dispatch but not his summons. This is the tusi (土变) pattern: enfeoffed by the court, autonomous at home, mustering its own troops, never attending court. His Grass-Head Soldiers outfight the heavenly regulars — as the Guangxi wolf-soldiers outfought the guard battalions. The paradox "semi-independent arms stronger than the regulars" is the deepest symptom of Ming military decay: when the state's regular army loses to half-independent private forces, the state's monopoly on violence is already nominal.
