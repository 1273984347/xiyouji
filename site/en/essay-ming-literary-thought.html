From Archaism to Xinling ↔ the spectrum

Written in the mid-to-late Ming (c. 1550s–1590s), the novel sits at the very hinge of Chinese literary thought's turn "from archaism to xinling (innate sensibility)." This essay places it in the Ming literary genealogy: laterally against the four great novels, and longitudinally against the early-Ming "cabinet style" (taiget i), the mid-Ming "Former and Latter Seven Masters" archaist movement, the Tang-Song School, the Gong'an School, and the Jingling School. The novel is a transitional text — it inherits the Tang-Song School's utilitarian "writing conveys the Way" yet anticipates the Gong'an School's "give voice to one's own xinling, bound by no formula," the optimistic prelude to a literature of emancipated individuality.

Four Literary Thinkers ↔ the scaffold

  • Li Zhi (1527–1602), On the Childlike Mind (Tongxin Shuo): the supreme literature issues from the "childlike mind" — the true, undefiled original mind. He condemns "received doctrines" that obscure it and breaks the genre hierarchy (poetry-prose over fiction), legitimising the novel and drama. His commentary on Water Margin opened the Ming practice of fiction commentary.
  • Yuan Hongdao (Gong'an School): "give voice to one's own xinling, bound by no formula" — oppose archaist mimicry ("prose must imitate Qin-Han, poetry must imitate High Tang"). A disciple lineage from Li Zhi; the Gong'an taste for popular literature prepared the soil for the novel's reception.
  • Gui Youguang (Tang-Song School): return to the natural fluency of the Eight Masters; "writing should flow from one's breast." His detail-realism (e.g. The Pavilion of Recollections) methodologically echoes the novel's everyday scene-writing and its rational social observation.
  • Li Mengyang (Former and Latter Seven Masters): "prose must imitate Qin-Han, poetry must imitate High Tang" — the orthodox "tonal-pattern" (gediao) formalism of mid-Ming. The novel's vernacular composition is itself a rebellion against this archaist hierarchy.

Rebel→Submit vs. Rebel→Surrender ↔ the contrast

Water Margin ends in tragic "rebel→surrender": the 108 are amnestied then decimated campaigning against Fang La. Journey ends in comic "rebel→submit": Wukong, crushed by the Five-Element Mountain, joins the pilgrimage and becomes a Buddha. At ch. 7, line 864 "the Great Sage escapes the Eight-Trigram Furnace" the rebellion peaks; at ch. 14, line 1393 "the mind-ape submits, the six thieves vanish" is the turning point; at ch. 100, line 7085 "the five pilgrims attain truth" is its completion. The difference refracts two Ming imaginings of resistance — a hopeless tragedy versus an optimistic comedy where individuality is preserved and sublimated through "submission."

A Transitional Text ↔ five stages

Early Ming (1368–1450) cabinet style · mid-Ming (1450–1520) the Seven Masters · mid-late (1520–1590) Tang-Song School / Childlike Mind · late (1590–1644) Gong'an / Jingling. The novel's composition period (c. 1550s–1590s) straddles the last two: it inherits the Tang-Song School's "writing conveys the Way" (ch. 1, line 522 "to know creation's work, read the Scripture of Liberation") yet anticipates the Gong'an "give voice to xinling" (Wukong's self-styled "Handsome Monkey King" and his "Equal-to-Heaven" title transgress rank). Yuan Hongdao praised the novel as "the strangest of all writings," confirming the Gong'an endorsement.
