Linjiangxian: The Fisherman-Oxherd's Hidden Realm

ESSAY · E17 · POETRY
The soft counter-meter to Xijiangyue — reclusion, contrast, and the only meter sung by a woodcutter's neighbour.
A curated English summary of the project's Chinese essay Xīyóu yǔ Línjiāngxiān Cípái Shǎngxī (A6 · W227). Theorists, dates, line anchors, and metric specs are taken from that source; no figures are invented. This is a summary translation, not a full rendering.

Introduction: The Fisherman and the Oxherd ↔ a lyric of reclusion

Of the novel's roughly twenty lyric-meter poems, the Linjiangxian (临江仙, "Immortal by the River") is the soft counterpart to the martial Xijiangyue. Where Xijiangyue frames battle musters, Linjiangxian frames the leisurely debate of a fisherman and an oxherd. Its prosody: shuangdiao, sixty characters, five lines per counterpart, three level-tone rhymes. The project's Chinese专题 Xīyóu yǔ Línjiāngxiān Cípái Shǎngxī (A6 · W227) reads the novel through four "realms" the meter produces — created, with-self, no-self, fulfilled — anchored at lines 981, 2306, 4432, and 7052.

Four Realms Across the Four Nodes ↔ line anchors

  • Created realm — ch. 8, line 981 (fisherman & oxherd). "The tide falls, I move the lonely boat... I arrange my heart as I please; the courtier stands cold awaiting the dial — how can that match my ease?" The fisherman builds a reclusive "created realm" to contrast the toil of office.
  • With-self realm — ch. 27, line 2306 (the White-Bone demon). Tang Seng, seeing with his "compassionate self," reads the demon as kindly; Wukong, with his "fiery-eye self," reads it as fiend. One demon, two colours — Wang Guowei's "things take the colour of my self." This with-self conflict splits the team.
  • No-self realm — ch. 58, line 4432 (the six-eared macaque). True and false monkey are "same in form, same in mind"; self and other dissolve. The demon is Wukong's own "second mind" — the lyric's "words end, meaning does not."
  • Fulfilled realm — ch. 99, line 7052 (eighty-one trials complete). The pilgrimage's labour "ends in words" (trials done) but its meaning "does not end" (scripture reaching the East) — the meter's lingering close, arrived at last.

Cultural Metaphor: Reclusion and the Ming Scholar's Spirit ↔ why it matters

The ch. 8 fisherman-oxherd exchange is rooted in the mid-late Ming psyche: amid factional strife and a corrupt court, scholars lived the "court-versus-wild" anxiety. Wu Cheng'en himself failed the exams and late became a minor prefectural aide — his frustration flows into the fisherman's "courtier cold at the dial / I at my ease." The Linjiangxian also belongs to the Ming ci revival: Yang Shen's "rolling river east, never to return" lifted the meter from Song "graceful boudoir" feeling to "meditative history." And the religious novel's use of a secular fisherman's lyric is a quiet irony — the sacred pilgrimage is launched in a secular, worldly key, proving the book always grounds the holy in Ming everyday life.

Conclusion: Soft Meter, Hard Contrast ↔ method

With Xijiangyue (firm) and Linjiangxian (soft), the novel's lyric pair is complete: one martial, one reclusive; one about becoming, one about contrast. The Chinese专题 treats the four realms — created, with-self, no-self, fulfilled — as a complete arc from ch. 8's opening造境 to ch. 99's closing圆成. For the English reader, the takeaway is that a "throwaway" fisherman's song in chapter 8 is doing structural work equal to any battle scene.
