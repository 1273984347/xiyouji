Ordination by Decree

ESSAY E27 · SUMMARY TRANSLATION
The scripture quest replays the Ming religious establishment — monk-officials, state cults, and ordination certificates as myth.
A summary translation of 明代宗教制度对照专题.md. The Chinese original reads the scripture quest as a precise literary replica of the Ming religious establishment — triangulated through Huang Renyu, Qian Mu, Max Weber, and Yang Qingkun.

Ordination by Decree ↔ the religious mirror

The scripture quest is not mere mythic travel; it is the literary replica of the Ming religious establishment. The Buddha convening the great assembly to launch the scripture project mirrors the court "ordaining a state rite"; Guanyin seeking a pilgrim mirrors the layered monk-official (儿录司) hierarchy; Xuanzang elevated by Taizong to preside over the Water-Land Assembly and granted the "monk-regulator" posts mirrors the four-tier Ming monk-official ranks; the Jade Emperor ruling the three realms mirrors the Ming state-cult apex of the "August Supreme God" (昂天上己); the Five attaining truth and their fruits "proclaimed" mirrors the state certification of monk-and-Daoist ranks through ordination certificates (度特) and bestowed Dharma-names. Written as Ming religion peaked yet festered — Jiajing's Daoist favouritism, Wanli's ruinous campaigns, Wudang and White Cloud Abbey court titles, Guandi absorbed into the state, ordination certificates degraded into purchasable commodities, the White Lotus rising.

Four Theorists ↔ the critical scaffold

  • Huang Renyu (1587, A Year of No Significance): Ming religion was "polity-primary, teaching-secondary" — religion fully absorbed as a governance tool, managed through the monk- and Daoist-registries, the sacrificial canon, ordination certificates, and the White Lotus ban. Jiajing's Daoist posturing was the throne's passive resistance to the civil service, not a real theocracy.
  • Qian Mu (Outline of National History): China had no state church but a "state sacrificial system" — the canon defined legitimate deities, the monk-official was a state bureaucrat, the ordination certificate a household register. The Ming monk-registry (儿录司) and Daoist-registry (道录司) doubled the celestial structure of Buddha-plus-Daoist patriarch.
  • Max Weber (The Religion of China): Chinese religion was "rationalised" into rites and bureaucracy — Confucianism, Daoism, and Buddhism all bureaucratised as extensions of the state, never producing a church independent of the throne. Guandi's canonisation is Weber's type-case of state co-optation of popular cults.
  • Yang Qingkun (Religion in Chinese Society): the institutional-diffused dichotomy — institutional religion (Buddhism, Daoism) with independent organisation versus diffused religion (ancestor, Guandi, city-god cults) permeating secular institutions. Ming diffused religion was nationalised: Guandi, the city-god, and Mazu pulled into the state canon.

The Pilgrim Team and the Monk-Official System ↔ Qian Mu

At ch. 12, line 1219 Xuanzang is elected by the monks and appointed by Taizong as "Left and Right Monk-Regulator, Universal Great Expounder" — the exact Ming procedure (monks recommend → the Ministry of Rites examines → the emperor appoints) and the apex rank of "Good-World" (唯世). Xuanzang's dual identity — monk and state bureaucrat — is the Ming monk-official's: a state agent presiding over a state rite. His "imperial-brother" (影弟) title at ch. 13 mirrors the Ming "state preceptor" (国师) honour — state-certified religious authority.

The Jade Emperor and the August Supreme God ↔ the state cult

The Jade Emperor as "highest god of the three realms" mirrors the Ming "August Supreme God" of the grand sacrifice to heaven — both symbols of supreme state theocracy. His authority is bounded by the Heavenly Law (rite), not by raw power, just as the Ming emperor was bound by "ancestral law." At ch. 5 the Monkey is excluded from the Peach Banquet — a low-rank deity shut out of state ritual, exactly as low-rank Ming officials were barred from the round-mound sacrifice.

Guandi and Guanyin ↔ the nationalised cults

Guandi's rise — from folk martial god to state "God of Loyalty and Righteousness" through a chain of Ming bestowals — mirrors Red Boy's absorption as the "Boy of Wealth" (ch. 42): folk sprites co-opted into the institutional pantheon. Guanyin as the project's "state agent" (seeking the pilgrim, granting the cassock, assessing merit) mirrors the Ming state's absorption of the Bodhisattva into a "state Bodhisattva." The city-god and the local Earth God (城陵 / 土地) — grassroots deities who "obey the Court" — are the nationalised diffused religion made myth: Yang Qingkun's "diffused religion nationalised" as the state's underworld grassroots bureaucracy.

The Eighty-One Trials and Ordination Assessment ↔ the certification

At ch. 99 Guanyin finds only eighty trials recorded and forces one more (the old tortoise) to complete eighty-one — "making up the number," structurally identical to the Ming monk-Daoist triennial assessment (考功: pass the classics, keep the precepts, be of good conduct, or lose the certificate). At ch. 100, line 7085 the Buddha proclaims the five fruits — Xuanzang the Sandalwood-Merit Buddha, Wukong the Victorious-Warrior Buddha, Bajie the Altar-Cleaner, Shaseng the Golden-Body Arhat, the dragon-horse the Eightfold Dragon — the "bestowed Dharma-name" ceremony, irreversible for life, exactly the Ming state's certification of religious authority. The tightening-band spell (紧篂周) is the precept-discipline tool enforcing compliance, applied asymmetrically — only to the unruly Wukong, never the compliant disciples.
