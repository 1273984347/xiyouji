Popular Religion in the Journey to the West

ESSAY · E12 · CULTURE
Beneath the Buddhist plot and Daoist grammar lies the book's true substrate: the diffuse, everyday religion of Ming villages.
A curated English summary of the project's Chinese essay Xīyóu yǔ Mínjiān Xìnyǎng Zhuān tí (A5 · W225). The four anthropologists and their concepts are taken from that source; no figures are invented. This is a summary translation, not a full rendering.

Introduction: A Folk-Religion Reading ↔ the hidden substrate

Popular religion (民间信仰) names the non-institutional, everyday religious practice — ancestor cults, local gods, demon-beliefs, geomancy, calendrical rites — that permeates family, community, and state. Unlike Chan or Quanzhen, it has no clergy, no headquarters, no systematic creed, yet it is the true ground of Chinese religious life. The novel's monsters, local gods, Tudigong (土地), Chenghuang (城隍), dragon-kings, and household protectors are not literati invention but literary photocopies of the Ming popular-religious world. The project's essay argues that this folk substrate — long hidden under "Buddhist enlightenment" and "Daoist elixir" readings — is the book's real mythic底色.

Four Core Concepts ↔ mapped onto the plot

  • Diffused religion (弥漫性宗教). The stone monkey's birth, the local Tudigong who report to heaven, the demons enshrined after capture — religion here is woven into everyday life, not confined to monastery or temple.
  • The imperial metaphor (帝国隐喻). Heaven's court, the dragon-kings' jurisdictions, the merit-reports sent up by burning tablets — the supernatural world is the Ming state administration mirrored and sacralised.
  • Gods / ghosts / ancestors (神鬼祖先三分). Protecting gods, threatening ghosts (the "stranger/refugee" Baigujing, who has no household, no register, no name), and patron-ancestors together form a cosmic sociology — Wukong the god expels Baigujing the ghost.
  • Standardisation (标准化). The pilgrimage's real work is not destroying demons but standardising them — turning wild local deities (Red Boy → Sudhana, Sha Wujing → Arhat) into empire-approved protectors.

Conclusion: The Book's True Mythic Ground ↔ why it matters

Read this way, the Journey to the West is neither purely Buddhist nor purely Daoist but popular religion wearing both costumes. Its monsters are folk demons, its gods are bureaucratised local protectors, its happy ending a canonisation rite. This folk lens is the necessary third term beside E10 (Quanzhen) and E11 (Chan): the elite frameworks explain the book's doctrine, but popular religion explains its life.
