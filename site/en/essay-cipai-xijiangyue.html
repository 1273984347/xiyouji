Xijiangyue: A Tiny Meter for Four Great Moments

ESSAY · E16 · POETRY
How a 52-character lyric meter secretly structures birth, ordeal, discipline, and apotheosis.
A curated English summary of the project's Chinese essay Xīyóu yǔ Xījiāngyuè Cípái Shǎngxī (A6 · W226). Theorists, dates, line anchors, and metric specs are taken from that source; no figures are invented. This is a summary translation, not a full rendering.

Introduction: A Lyric Meter as a Hidden Spine ↔ the prosody beneath the prose

The Journey to the West carries more than eight hundred poems; of these, only about twenty are written to a named cipai (a fixed lyric meter). The Xijiangyue ("Moon over the West River") is the classic xiaoling — the short lyric. Its prosody is exact: shuangdiao (two Counterparts), fifty-two characters, four lines per counterpart, level-tone rhymes. The novel never prints the words "Xijiangyue" in its chapter titles, yet the spirit of that meter threads through four pivotal nodes — the stone monkey's birth, the Bagua furnace, the Band-Tightening spell, and the five pilgrims' apotheosis — tracing a covert path of created realm → no-self realm → with-self realm → fulfilled realm. This essay follows the project's Chinese essay Xīyóu yǔ Xījiāngyuè Cípái Shǎngxī (A6 · W226) in reading those four moments as a single lyric arc.

Four Realms Across the Four Nodes ↔ line anchors

  • Created realm — ch. 1, line 522 (stone monkey born). "A fairy stone, split, yields a stone egg; the wind makes it a stone monkey." This is Wang Guowei's created realm: not observed nature but an ideal construction — heaven-and-earth imagining a monkey into being.
  • No-self realm — ch. 7, line 864 (the Bagua furnace). Forty-nine days in Laozi's furnace burn Wukong into the Fiery Eyes. He "looks at things with things," not knowing which is self and which is furnace — the textbook no-self realm.
  • With-self realm — ch. 14, line 1393 (the Band-Tightening spell). Guanyin's spell lets Tang Seng discipline the mind-monkey: "I look at things with my self, so all things take my colour." The band's pain is the with-self realm — and Ye Jiaying's "beauty of weak virtue" under constraint.
  • Fulfilled realm — ch. 100, line 7085 (the five become perfected). Wukong, crowned Victorious Fighting Buddha, reaches up and the band is simply gone — with-self and no-self fused, created and depicted merged. The lyric closes on its final rhyme.

Cultural Metaphor: The Ming Decline of Ci, and Its Survival ↔ why it matters

The novel appears in the mid-to-late Ming, exactly the period Xia Chengtao calls the "decline of ci": meters loosened, the song-form (qu) pulled talent away from lyric, and many poems lost their evocative charge. Yet Wu Cheng'en folds the spirit of Xijiangyue into narrative — a quiet "preserving ci through the novel." Three further echoes ring through the meter: the Ming "heart-school" of Wang Yangming maps onto the four realms (mind-as-principle, innate knowing, the unity of knowledge and action, fulfilled conscience); the three-teachings synthesis shows in the meter's two counterparts (Dao-Buddhist duality) and level rhymes (harmony); and the era's print boom spread cipai manuals even as it hollowed them out — Wu answers hollow form with living feeling.

Conclusion: A Lyric Hiding in Plain Sight ↔ method

Read as a Xijiangyue, the novel's four great moments are not isolated spectacles but one fifty-two-character arc — birth, refinement, discipline, fulfillment. The Chinese essay argues that the novel's poetry is best understood not as decoration but as a "form-rhythm axis" beside the "shape-axis" of its scenery verse. For English readers this means: when Wukong is born, burned, bound, and crowned, you are also hearing a four-line lyric the author never wrote down but never stopped singing.
