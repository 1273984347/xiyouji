Three Teachings, One Journey: Buddhist, Daoist, and Confucian Thought

ESSAY · E15 · THOUGHT
Born at the saturation point of Ming syncretism — a Buddhist plot over a Daoist-Chan grammar, in a Confucian frame.
A curated English summary of the project's Chinese essay Fódào Sīxiǎng (A5 · Three Teachings Unity Origins · four thought sources). The skandha→pilgrim table and all doctrinal claims are taken from that source; no figures are invented. This is a summary translation, not a full rendering.

Introduction: Born at the Saturation Point ↔ three teachings, one book

The project's Chinese study Fódào Sīxiǎng reads the novel as the literary precipitate of three-teachings-syncretism at its Ming peak. Not one school's victory, but three traditions seeping into and borrowing shapes from one another. The book's "confusion" — its gods and Buddhas indistinguishable — is the shadow of its age. This essay follows the source across Buddhism, Daoism, and Confucianism as they surface in the hundred chapters.

The Age of Synthesis ↔ Jiajing–Wanli

Under Jiajing and Wanli, the old separation of the three teachings loosened. The emperor favoured Daoism; Buddhism's official rank fell while folk devotion boomed; Wang Yangming's "heart learning" spread the idea that "the mind is principle." Thinkers crossed boundaries — Lin Zhao'en founded a "Three-in-One" teaching; Li Zhi preached "childlike mind"; Jiao Hong and Yuan Hongdao read Chan through Confucius and Confucius through Chan. The novel's ambiguity and tension come straight from this saturation: it will not, and cannot, exalt a single household.

Buddhism ↔ the eighty-one as spiritual stages

The scripture-fetching theme is Buddhist at root: the eighty-one tribulations are the literary form of spiritual progression, each ordeal a barrier broken, each barrier a delusion dissolved — "the ordeal is not outside, it is in the mind," the core Chan claim. "Mind-monkey" and "will-horse" are classic Chan metaphors: the mind like a clambering ape, the will like a runaway steed. Wukong is the mind-monkey, the White Dragon Horse the will-horse — the inner landscape of the journey. Tathagata, Guanyin, and Maitreya form a three-layer Buddhist pantheon (present, future, saviour-Bodhisattva); and, in a hidden key, the five skandhas map onto the five pilgrims:

The five skandhas are one person; one person divided is the pilgrimage team. To fetch the scriptures is, at bottom, the self's delivering of its own five aggregates.

Daoism ↔ inner alchemy in the cast

Subodhi wears a Buddhist name but Daoist robes, dwelling at "Spirit-Terrace, Square-Inch Mountain, Slant-Moon, Three-Star Cave" — "spirit-terrace" and "square-inch" both mean the mind, and "slant-moon three-star" spells the character heart(xin). This both-and teacher is syncretism made flesh. The whole pantheon is laced with inner-alchemy terms: Wukong is "Metal Sire" (Jingong, the metal of geng), Bajie "Wood Mother" (Mumu, the wood of yi), Sha "Yellow Dame" (Huangpo, the earth of wu-ji) — the alchemical "three meet" in which metal, wood, and earth conjoin to form the elixir. The Quanzhen school's commentaries (the Qing Zhengdao and Zhenquan editions) read the entire book as an inner-alchemy fable — every chapter a method, every demon a mental obstacle. Whether the author was an alchemist remains an open question, but the book can certainly be read as a cinnabar classic.

Confucianism ↔ master-disciple as lord-son

The master-disciple bond mirrors the Confucian ruler-subject / father-son structure. Tang Sanzang as teacher is father; Wukong as disciple is son. That Wukong keeps calling him "master" — a "father" not used loosely — lands Confucian ethics precisely. The monk's "loyalty to the emperor" (sent by Taizong) and the monkey's "loyalty to the master" (returning though thrice expelled) drape a religious quest under political order. Tellingly, even Buddhist scripture-fetching requires the emperor's word — the Confucian disciplining of the Buddhist, written into the premise itself.

Conclusion ↔ a book that refuses to choose

The novel is neither Buddhist nor Daoist nor Confucian alone — it is their mingling, the literary form of a Ming moment when belief's borders first blurred. That is why this English edition pairs E10 (Quanzhen Daoism), E11 (Chan Buddhism), and this essay: together they show a book wearing a Buddhist plot over a Daoist-Chan grammar, disciplined throughout by a Confucian frame.
