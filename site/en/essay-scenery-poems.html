Original Scenery Poems

ESSAY · SUMMARY TRANSLATION
Mountains of wonder, roadside lyrics, and the sacred terminus — scenery as a hidden pilgrimage of the mind.
Source: A summary translation of Original Scenery Poem Classification Appreciation (essay, Chinese). The original Chinese essay classifies the novel's eighty-odd scenery poems by their narrative function. This English version preserves the three-type scheme and verified examples (each cross-checked against the chapter source text).

From Listing to Typology ↔ function over decoration

The novel contains some eighty to ninety scenery poems. A line-by-line catalog risks "seeing trees but not the forest." This essay instead sorts them by the lyrical function they serve in the narrative into three types: wonder-landscape poems (the mythic space's grandeur and order, concentrated at the opening); wayfaring poems (the journey's travel-anxiety and scenery-as-mind, scattered along the road); and chan-sacred poems (the Buddha-land's sanctity and cultivation-terminus, closing at Vulture Peak). The gain is that scenery is no longer a static prop but a lyrical device that flows with the narrative. The same "green pines and cypresses" is an order-proclamation of the "ancestral dragon-vein" at the opening, a lonely comfort for travel-weariness midway, and a symbol of terminus at Vulture Peak.

· · ·

Wonder-Landscape: Legislating the Mythic Space ↔ opening flourish

The first type is the opening's expansive parallel-prose fu. They do not describe one roadside scene but "legislate" the whole mythic world — with dense parallelism turning a mountain or sea into an "as-it-ought-to-be" order. Chapter 1's Flower-Fruit Mountain fu is the model: "Its power guards the vast ocean, its awe calms the jade sea… on cinnabar cliffs phoenixes cry in pairs; before the sheer wall a unicorn lies alone." Imagery stacks mountain, sea, auspicious beasts, and immortal plants, writing the mountain as the cosmic pivot of "ten isles' ancestral vein." Technique: four-character parallel fu at maximum density — "cinnabar cliff" against "sheer wall," "phoenix" against "unicorn." Function: evocation plus the establishment of spatial order — "guards" and "calms" are an order-proclamation, setting the tone for the book's sacred geography. By contrast, same-chapter Spirit-Terrace Fangcun Mountain adds "withered vine," "old ferry," "hidden path" and a "cold" hue — a "Daoist mountain" of solitary seeking, functioning as point-scene plus transition as Wukong moves from natural immortality to cultivation.

· · ·

Wayfaring Poems: Scenery as the Mind ↔ travel lyric

The second type is the short roadside poem. Brief and mobile, its function is not legislation but "writing the mind" — steep mountains for danger, deep waters for obstruction, night-lodging for loneliness; scenery externalizes inner state. Chapter 22's Quicksand River poem writes water-danger: "Eight hundred leagues of quicksand, three thousand fathoms of weak water; a goose-feather cannot float, a reed-flower sinks to the bottom." Number-parallelism and the paradoxical image of things light sinking turn the mythic "weak water" into felt dread. Function: point-scene plus rendering obstruction, and more deeply "scenery as mind" — the unfordable water is the unresolvable anxiety of the pilgrimage. Chapter 36's Baolin Temple poem is the dignified loneliness of night-lodging: an architectural fu opening with "eight-character brick walls daubed red, gold nails on both gates," then dense parallels of pavilions and halls. The temple's splendor contrasts the hardship of the road, and its quiet ("green pines by the bamboo court, clear every meditation hall") is the medicine for travel-weariness — a classic contrast device: the purer the scene, the sharper the contrast with the monk-warden's impure reception.

· · ·

Chan-Sacred Poems: Sanctity and the Terminus ↔ closure

The third type writes the Buddha-land's solemn purity; its function is closure plus terminus — gathering the whole road's landscape and travel into the sacred. Notably, Chapter 52's Vulture Peak poem appears mid-journey: when Wukong first ascends to ask the Buddha about the yaksha-rider demon, the pure realm is already described — "spirit peaks sparse and grand, layered ranges clear and fine… green pines beneath which upāsakas expound, blue cypresses between which arhats walk." This plants the "terminus" image early; paired with Chapter 1's Flower-Fruit Mountain fu ("guards and calms" spatial grandeur), the "completion" of Vulture Peak is narratively foreshadowed. Chapter 1's Water-Curtain Cave interior poem is the "sacred dwelling of the cultivation's start": "mist-and-rosy-clouds ever shine, auspicious vapor ever steams; pines and bamboos flourish year by year, strange flowers renew day by day." With no Buddha-statue yet auspicious vapor, it is a "natural-cave chan-realm"; opposite Vulture Peak's jeweled hall, the two form a net-realm counterpoint — the cave Wukong departs from and the peak he arrives at already echo each other on the dimension of "purity."

· · ·

Conclusion: A Hidden Pilgrimage of Scenery ↔ mind-path isomorphism

The typological insight: wonder-landscape establishes "the order of space," wayfaring writes "the mind of the road," chan-sacred returns "the fruit of cultivation." Head and tail interlocking, the scenery poems themselves form an invisible pilgrimage. Three types can be mapped onto the pilgrim's mind-path: wonder is the "grandeur of original mind," wayfaring is the "process of scenery-as-mind," chan-sacred is the "completion of terminus." The classification of scenery poems is isomorphic with the cultivator's inner journey — not a person walking, but scenery speaking the mind for him. The original never writes scenery realistically; it always writes toward the immortal and pure (Water-Curtain Cave turns from "wet" to "mist-clouds," Quicksand River from "water" to "weak-water deep"). This "immortalizing, not realistic" tradition gives scenery poems a dual lyric-and-symbolic function — worth contrasting with contemporary "landscape writing."
