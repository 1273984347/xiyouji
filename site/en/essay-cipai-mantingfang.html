Mantingfang: The Only Named Tune in the Book

ESSAY · E18 · POETRY
Chapter 1, line 39 — a woodcutter names the tune aloud. The genre's single native anchor.
A curated English summary of the project's Chinese essay Xīyóu yǔ Mǎntíngfāng Cípái Shǎngxī (A6 · W228). Theorists, dates, line anchors, and metric specs are taken from that source; no figures are invented. This is a summary translation, not a full rendering.

Introduction: The Only Named Tune in the Whole Book ↔ a unique anchor

Among the novel's lyric-meter poems, exactly one is named aloud by a character: in chapter 1, line 39, the woodcutter sings and says, "this song is called Mantingfang" (满庭芳, "Courtyard Full of Fragrance"). It is the only explicitly named cipai in the entire 100-chapter novel — the genre's native anchor point. The project's Chinese专题 Xīyóu yǔ Mǎntíngfāng Cípái Shǎngxī (A6 · W228) treats that moment as origin: from it, the meter's four realms — created, with-self, no-self, fulfilled — map onto four key nodes (lines 39, 981, 4792, 7085).

Four Realms Across the Four Nodes ↔ line anchors

  • Created realm — ch. 1, line 39 (the woodcutter). "Watching chess, the ax-handle rots; hewing wood, ding-ding... I sell fuel, buy wine, laugh freely, content myself." Not a depiction of real poverty but a constructed reclusive ideal — Wang Guowei's "created realm," anchored by the only spoken tune-name in the book.
  • With-self realm — ch. 8, line 981 (the mission launched). The Buddha vows to send scripture east, "looking at things with my self" — the "great self" of a saviour, contrasting the woodcutter's "small self." The mission is with-self on a cosmic scale.
  • No-self realm — ch. 64, line 4792 (the Tree-Spirit poetry gathering). At Thorn-Ridge, Tang Seng trades verses with pine, cypress, juniper, bamboo, apricot, plum, and cassia spirits — "looking at things with things," self and other dissolved. The spell is broken only when the apricot sprite presses marriage — the no-self realm is fragile in reality.
  • Fulfilled realm — ch. 100, line 7085 (the five become perfected). From the woodcutter's created realm, through the Buddha's with-self and the trees' no-self, to apotheosis — the meter's four realms close into a ring.

Cultural Metaphor: The Woodcutter's Folkness ↔ why it matters

Choosing a woodcutter as the meter's bearer is deliberate. In Chinese culture the woodcutter is the go-between of "secular and transcendent" — from the Book of Songs' "hewing wood, ding-ding" to the immortal-tale of Wang Zhi watching immortals play chess until his ax-handle rots. Wu Cheng'en lets a woodcutter sing Mantingfang, sinking the elite lyric into folk song: in the mid-late Ming, with rising urban culture, meters moved from the scholar's studio to the commoner's "heart-easing" recitation. The woodcutter even says the song was "taught me by an immortal" — folk belief fused with lyric culture. The book also opens a "meter-meets-fiction" conversation: by naming the tune aloud, it prefigures the later Dream of the Red Chamber's use of lyric meters inside fiction.

Conclusion: The Named Origin of a Wordless Tradition ↔ method

Mantingfang is the "origin point" of the novel's lyric reading precisely because it is the one meter spoken by name. From that anchor the Chinese专题 builds the full "created → with-self → no-self → fulfilled" path and closes the A6 three-essay cipai sequence (Xijiangyue · Linjiangxian · Mantingfang). For English readers the lesson is simple but striking: a woodcutter's offhand "this is called Mantingfang" is the key that unlocks a four-realm architecture running the length of the book.
