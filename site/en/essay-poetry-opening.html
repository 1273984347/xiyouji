The Opening Verse — Poetics of the Frame

ESSAY E31 · SUMMARY TRANSLATION
Six paired-couplet chapter titles (chs. 1/7/8/14/22/100) fold the Three Teachings into eight characters each — a 'constitutional' poetics.
A summary translation of Opening Poems In-Depth (essay, Chinese). The Chinese original reads the novel's verse through three poet theorists and six frame couplets (E31), three classes of scene-poem (E32), and four imagery theorists reading a four-image lineage (E33). All line numbers and chapter titles are carried over from the source without fabrication.

Three Poetic Coordinates ↔ Wang · Zhu · Ye

The opening-verse study builds three poetic coordinates. Wang Guowei (Wang Guowei's Renjian Cihua, the "realm" theory) supplies the "self-involved" vs "self-removed" scene — the couplet's power to "build a realm" (zaojing). Zhu Guangqian (Zhu's Poetics) supplies "the poetic realm = intuition + affection + image," asking how imagery carries the author's felt interest. Ye Jiaying (Ye's Ganfa theory of "evocation and moved response," the beauty of passive virtue) supplies the "arousal" mechanism — how eight characters ignite a hundred chapters of narrative expectation.

Ch. 1 — "Linggen yuyun yuanliu chu" ↔ line 522

"Linggen yuyun yuanliu chu · Xinxing xiuchi dadao sheng" (A numinous root breeds the source-stream; mind-nature cultivates the Great Way). Eight characters hide three teachings' terms: linggen (numinous root) is Daoist (the innate true nature, the Flower-Fruit stone); xinxing (mind-nature) is Buddhist (the Chan ontological ground); dadao (Great Way) is Confucian (the political-ethical ideal). The three stand side by side, none dominant — Wu Cheng'en's foundational declaration that the book is not one teaching's novel but a three-teachings-synthesis narrative. Read through Wang Guowei, the first five characters are a self-removed realm (objective birth of a stone-monkey); the last five are self-involved (the subjective will in "cultivate") — from self-removed to self-involved, exactly Wukong's arc from innocent stone to enlightened pilgrim.

Ch. 7 — "Bagua lu zhong tao dasheng" ↔ line 864

"Bagua lu zhong tao Dasheng · Wuxing shan xia ding Xinyuan" (The Great Sage escapes the Eight-Trigram furnace; the mind-monkey is pinned beneath the Five-Phases mountain). The couplet hides a wuxing (Five-Phases) conquest structure: fire (furnace) subdues metal (the Monkey, "Metal Sire"); yet the Monkey survives and forges the Fiery Eyes — metal counters fire. Earth (the mountain) drains fire (the restless mind-monkey belongs to fire). A complete wuxing cycle — fire → metal → earth → fire — rendered as literary philosophy.

Ch. 8 — "Wofo zaojing chuan jile" ↔ line 982

"Wo Fo zao jing chuan jile · Guanyin fengzhi shang Chang'an" (Buddha makes scriptures to spread bliss; Guanyin takes the edict to Chang'an). Three verbs build a power gradient: zaojing (make) — the Buddha's sovereign act of authoring the canon; chuan jile (spread bliss) — his act of exporting the Pure Land; fengzhi (take the edict) — Guanyin's subordinate act. Buddha (maker) → Buddha (spreader) → Guanyin (edict-bearer): a poetic diagram of the Buddhist hierarchy compressed into eight characters — the Dao opens at ch. 1, the Buddha takes over at ch. 8.

Ch. 14 — "Xinyuan guizheng" ↔ line 1394

"Xinyuan gui zheng · Liuzei wuzong" (The mind-monkey returns to right; the six thieves vanish). Xinyuan (mind-monkey) is a Chan core image (from the Vimalakirti Sutra: "the hard-to-tame mind is like a monkey"). Wukong receiving the fillet is not physical restraint but a Xin-Xue (Heart-Mind learning) "gathering of the heart." The six thieves are the six sense-roots' afflictions; Wukong's slaying of them is a violent narrative carrying the Chan "six roots purified" metaphor. "Return to right" (inward) vs "thieves vanish" (outward) is the poetic projection of Wang Yangming's "easy to crush mountain-thieves, hard to crush heart-thieves."

Ch. 22 — "Bajie dazhan Liusha he" ↔ line 1936

"Bajie dazhan Liusha He · Mucha fengfa shou Wujing" (Bajie battles the Quicksand River; Moksha, by the Dharma, receives Wujing). The team's five-person roster completes here. The three-beat "battle — mediation — recruitment" (Bajie fights, Moksha intervenes, Wujing is taken) is the poetic arc of team assembly — each new member repeats the same beat (Wukong via Guanyin, Bajie via Guanyin, Wujing via Moksha-by-Guanyin's law).

Ch. 100 — "Jinghui dongtu" ↔ line 7086

"Jing hui Dongtu · Wusheng chengzhen" (Straight back to the Eastern Land; the Five Sages become perfected). "Eastern Land" is the Confucian ethical world (Great Tang); "become perfected" (chengzhen) is Daoist (Perfected Ones), "sage" (sheng) is Confucian, "five" is the wuxing number. The eight characters fuse Buddha (return), Dao (perfected), Confucian (sage) — echoing ch. 1's opening and closing the ring.

The Power of the Three Teachings ↔ Dao → Buddha → synthesis

Across the six key frame couplets, the three teachings' discourse shifts dynamically: ch. 1 & 7 Dao-led (root, furnace, mountain); ch. 8, 14, 22 Buddha-led (make-scripture, mind-monkey, take-by-Dharma); ch. 100 fused. The trajectory — Dao (chs. 1–7) → Buddha (chs. 8–99) → synthesis (ch. 100) — is the literary map of Ming three-teachings synthesis. Wang Yangming's Xin-Xue (mature c. 1500–1520) and the book's composition (c. 1570–1590) are near-contemporary; ch. 14's resonance with "crush the heart-thief" is no accident — the frame-verse series is Ming Heart-Mind learning projected onto the chapter titles.
