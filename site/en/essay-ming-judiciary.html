Justice in Three Realms

ESSAY E23 · SUMMARY TRANSLATION
Four cases — a quiet crime, a repentant king, a silenced queen, a forged ledger — expose Ming law's fault lines.
A summary translation of Ming Judiciary In-Depth (essay, Chinese). The Chinese original reads four novel cases as a deepened mirror of Ming jurisprudence — triangulated through Huang Rensong, Qu Tongzu, Shiga Shūzō, and Terada Hiroaki.

Three Realms, Three Courts ↔ the judiciary map

Ming justice rested on the "three judicial offices" (三法司: Ministry of Justice, Censorate, Court of Judicial Review) with provincial and prefectural courts below. The novel maps this exactly: Heaven = the three offices (Jade Emperor = emperor, Li Jing = ministry, Taibai = review court), the Underworld = provincial authority (Ten Kings = surveillance commissioner, Judge Cui = clerk), the human kingdoms = prefectures (Zizhu, Wuji, Bhikṣu = counties). Three deep traits: 祖法合一 (ritual fused with law), 情理法 (feeling → reason → law, in that order), and 窆抵伸理 (redress of grievance, not rights-protection). Companion to the Chinese project's W078 "judicial mirror" topic.

Case 1 — the "Quiet Criminal" (赛太山) ↔ exile penalty

The Golden-King steals Guanyin's bell, abducts Queen Jingu, then locks himself in his cave — no expansion, no eating people. This "quiet crime" has no Ming statutory match, yet mirrors the exile penalty (流刑): far from home, settled in place, no return — a self-imposed exile. His biting through the chain (逃粉) mirrors the "deserting soldier" (逃军). When Guanyin simply "recalls" him, no trial — Huang Rensong's "morality substituting for law": the master-servant ethic overwrites the robbery charge.

Case 2 — the Repentant King (朱素国) ↔ the guilt-edict

The Zizhu king's "crime" (wounding a peacock in a past life) is never litigated; it becomes a three-year wine-waiting ritual of repentance. This mirrors the Ming emperor's guilt-edict (罪己谈) — a political gesture, not a legal procedure; after self-accusation, related cases are "leniently released." The king's repentance suspends the demon's prosecution: if the king admits karma, the demon is karma's executor — to prosecute the demon is to deny cosmic order. Qu Tongzu's 祖法合一: the king's crime dissolved by ritual (causal retribution), never judged by law; royal power is simultaneously judge, judged, and pardoner.

Case 3 — the Silenced Queen (金址宗) ↔ women's law

Queen Jingu is "protected" by a spiky brown robe (棕衣) for three years — chastity preserved, but she loses the right to refuse, even the right to touch herself. The robe protects the king's face, the state's propriety, the ritual order — never Jingu herself. This is Ming women's legal "objectification" (客体化): wives had no independent standing, "a woman's word" was discounted, victimisation meant social stigma. Her choosing silence is the mythic version of the female litigant's "voicelessness" (失声) — grievance that cannot be translated into relief.

Case 4 — the Forged Ledger (嵵判官) ↔ institutional corruption

Emperor Taizong's underworld trial: Wei Zheng's letter pulls strings; Judge Cui alters the life-register (生死膨: "13" → "33"); the Ten Kings collectively stamp approval; the mirror of deeds (业镱) is never activated; the wronged Jiancheng and Yuanji are "pushed into the City of Wrongful Death." This is the full chain of Ming institutional corruption — clerks manipulating records (书变操式), collective "review" endorsement (会审背书), and justice hollowed by power (谱状). Terada's "grievance must be heard to count" is inverted: heard, yet pushed aside, never tried.

Conclusion ↔ fault lines of Ming law

Four cases — criminal law, church-state, gender, administration — expose the Ming legal order's fault lines: stagnation by morality-over-law, hierarchical 祖法合一, distorted 情理法, and grievance silenced by power. From the quiet criminal to the forged ledger, the novel is not telling fairy tales but re-narrating Ming justice's deepest pathologies in myth.
