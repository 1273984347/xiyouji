The Making of the Novel: Authorship, Texts, and the Ming World

ESSAY · E13 · COMPOSITION
Who wrote it, which text we read, and the Ming world that shaped a four-hundred-year bestseller.
A curated English summary of the project's Chinese essay Chéngshū Bèijǐng (A5 · Culture and History Background). All dates, the version lineage, and the historical/fictional Xuanzang comparison are taken from that source; no figures are invented. This is a summary translation, not a full rendering.

Introduction: A Masterpiece Without a Certain Author ↔ the puzzle

The Journey to the West is one of the four great classical novels of China, yet its authorship is unusually contested for a book of its stature. What we hold today — a hundred-chapter vernacular novel — is the product of centuries of accretion by anonymous storytellers and a final redactor whose identity the academy still debates. This essay follows the project's Chinese study Chéngshū Bèijǐng through the three questions every reader should know: who wrote it, which text we actually read, and what world shaped it.

The Authorship Puzzle ↔ Wu Cheng'en and the doubters

The conventional attribution is to Wu Cheng'en (c. 1500–1582), a literatus of Huai'an (Huai'an Shanyang, in modern Jiangsu), styled Ruzhong, styled Sheyang Shanren. The claim rests mainly on the Tianqi-era Huai'an Prefecture Gazetteer, whose bibliography lists "Wu Cheng'en — Sheyang Collection in four juan, Preface to the Spring and Autumn Annals Biographies, and the Journey to the West." Lu Xun adopted this in his History of Chinese Fiction, and it became canonical.

Since the 1980s scholars such as Zhuang Peiheng have raised doubts: the gazetteer entry might refer to a travelogue rather than the novel; the earliest complete edition — the Shide Hall print — names no author, bearing only the editor's seal "Master of the Huayang Grotto"; and the book's heavy northern dialect and Daoist inner-alchemy jargon sit oddly with a southern man of letters. Competing candidates include Qiu Chuji (Qiu Chuji, the Quanzhen patriarch — a Qing-era attribution now rejected), the "Master of the Huayang Grotto" himself (identity unknown), and the collective-accumulation theory — that no single author exists because the story grew from centuries of popular oral and written performance. The mainstream today holds a compromise: Wu Cheng'en as final redactor, upon earlier accretions.

The Shide Hall edition (full title Newly Cut, Illustrated, Official-Format, Large-Character Journey to the West) is the closest to the original face of the book, and it is the project's own base text. Known lacunae exist in its scrolls 3, 9, 13, 15, and 18, which later collations supply from other editions.

The leap from a solitary historian-monk to a team-escorted fiction is a complete remaking from history into myth. It crystallised after the Southern-Song Scripture-Fetching Tale and was fixed in the Ming Shide Hall redaction — the very transformation this project's E7 essay examines in depth.

The Ming World That Made It ↔ three teachings, one market

Wu Cheng'en lived under the Jiajing and Wanli emperors, the high tide of three-teachings-syncretism: the Zhengde-Jiajing court favoured Daoism (the emperor's private altar burned day and night), Buddhism thrived in folk practice even as its official standing fell, and Wang Yangming's "heart learning" ("the mind is principle") spread through the literati. The novel's Buddhist-Daoist interpenetration and its "mind-monkey" vocabulary answer directly to that moment. Equally, the Ming bureaucracy it mirrors (the real Office of the Imperial Stables behind "Bimawen"; the nine-rank officialdom behind Heaven's hierarchy; demons "with backing reclaimed, without smashed") and the booming vernacular novel born of a rising urban class — all are products of the mid-to-late Ming.

Conclusion ↔ why the uncertainty matters

To read the novel well is to hold two truths at once: it is a popular oral epic centuries in the making, polished by a Ming redactor we may never name with certainty; and the text we read is the 1592 Shide Hall print, not the Tang pilgrim's world. That humility about origin is itself a reading method — and it frames every essay in this English edition.
