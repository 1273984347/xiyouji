The Sea Ban Made the Ocean a Frontier

ESSAY E22 · SUMMARY TRANSLATION
HuaGuo Mountain as lawless offshore space; the sanctioned voyage as tribute trade.
A summary translation of 明代海禁政策对照专题.md. The Chinese original reads the 'cross-sea scripture quest' as a literary projection of the Ming maritime ban (海禁) era — sanctioned voyage vs. forbidden private sea — triangulated through Huang Rensong, Fan Shuzhi, Li Qing, and Timothy Brook.

The Banned Sea and the Imagined Sea ↔ the haijin thesis

The novel was written during the sea-ban's great upheaval: Hongwu's strict prohibition (1371), Jiajing's peak repression (1522–1566) that bred the "Japanese-bandit" (像宝) crisis, the Longqing "switch-on" (雅庆开关, 1567) at Yuegang, and late-Ming relaxation. HuaGuo Mountain as an "offshore immortal mountain" (ch. 1, line 522) is the literary projection of an "overseas lawless space" born of the ban. The ban severed material exchange but stimulated an "overseas imagination" — exactly why so many late-Ming novels use the sea as narrative space.

Four Line Anchors ↔ banned · sanctioned · smuggled · reflux

  • Ch. 1, line 522 — HuaGuo Mountain: "ancestor of the ten isles," an overseas realm beyond the Jade Emperor's direct rule; Wukong's self-enthronement there mirrors the ban's creation of offshore lawless zones where pirates and smugglers gathered.
  • Ch. 8, line 996 — the Buddha initiates the quest: Guanyin executes, Tripitaka bears it — a "state-sanctioned crossing." Tripitaka's passport (通关文犯) stamped at each kingdom = the tribute "tally" (矿合); certification is the precondition of lawful crossing.
  • Ch. 22, line 1936 — Flowing-Sand River: crossing with Moksha's help is a "semi-official crossing," mirroring private maritime passage that needed certification yet remained unofficial.
  • Ch. 100, line 7085 — scriptures eastward: "cultural cross-sea reflux." The ban forbade material trade but never stopped cultural crossing — Buddhism, Islam, Christianity all crossed in the cracks.

Four Historians ↔ the sea-ban lens

  • Huang Rensong (1587): the ban as the necessary reverse of the tribute-trade system (朝賂–海禁) — "politics substituting for economy"; the sanctioned voyage as mythic tribute trade.
  • Fan Shuzhi (Late Ming History): the ban's tragedy — "prohibit yet cannot stop"; the stricter the ban, the fiercer the smuggling; the "Japanese bandits" were mostly "fake Japanese" (假像) — coastal fishermen and merchants bankrupted by the ban. HuaGuo Mountain's "outlaw monkey" as the product of ungoverned offshore space; Wukong's later amnesty mirrors the court's co-opting of pirates (e.g. Zheng Zhilong).
  • Li Qing (History of Ming Overseas Trade): the ban's effectiveness was always zero — trade moved underground (Shuangyu port, etc.); the novel's "crossing waters" as mythic private maritime passage.
  • Timothy Brook (The Confusions of Pleasure): the ban intensified desire for overseas luxuries (pepper / ivory / coral) by forbidding them; the Dragon Palace's "borrowed treasure" (金篂棱 etc.) as mythic overseas luxury; the true scripture itself as an "overseas cultural luxury."

Jurisdiction vs. Flow ↔ the ban's contradiction

The ban's core contradiction — fixed jurisdiction trying to control fluid ocean — was doomed. HuaGuo Mountain as lawless offshore space shows that over-prohibition breeds lawless space (drug bans breed black markets; the sea ban bred pirates). The Longqing "switch-on" — governing by opening rather than forbidding — is the historical prelude to "fluid governance." The scripture's eastward transmission remains a mythic plea: cultural crossing cannot be banned.
