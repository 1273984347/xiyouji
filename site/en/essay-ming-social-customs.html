Customs as Governance ↔ Foucault

Ming social customs were not scattered habits; they were institutionalised customs underwritten by the Great Ming Code — every custom had a statute behind it and carried the throne's governance of the body. The novel was written across the Jiajing–Wanli arc, exactly when Ming mores lurched from "Hongwu frugality" to "late-Ming extravagance," so its writing of custom necessarily carries the era's veiled projection. Michel Foucault's notion of gouvernementalité (governmentality) tells us: custom is the throne's most hidden discipline of the body — it internalises power through daily practice, more effective than law. Beneath the god-and-demon shell, the novel completes a literary archive of Ming custom-politics.

Each of the five dimensions is a technology of power: marriage disciplines the sexual body (the chastity arch internalises female submission); dress disciplines the status body (rank internalises social difference); diet disciplines the desiring body (food-as-medicine internalises the regimen of health); burial disciplines the living-dead body (rite internalises fear of and submission to death); the exam disciplines the knowing body (the eight-legged essay internalises thought-norms).

Marriage: Chastity and Its Monsters ↔ ch. 54 / 55 / 82

The Kingdom of Women (ch. 54, line 4145) is the most peculiar "no-demon ordeal": the Tang Monk faces not a fiend but a queen who voluntarily proposes marriage. Her "willingness to give a kingdom's wealth to take him as consort" — reversed by his refusal — is the inverted mirror of the Ming chastity-arch system. The Hongwu throne had decreed that widows who kept faith be honoured at the gate; Chen Baoliang (History of Ming Social Life) notes chastity ideals peaked in the Ming, with tens of thousands of exemplary women recorded. The queen's "active proposal" is the maximal transgression of that system; the Monk's refusal is its literary reinforcement.

The Scorpion Spirit (ch. 55, line 4231) seduces the Tang Monk; the chapter title names it "lustful evil." This naming strategy is the literary projection of Ming repression of female desire — the Neixun and Guifan codified "chaste stillness" as the core of womanly virtue, and active female eroticism was doubly negated: morally as "licentious," literarily as "demon." Her death at the Rooster-spirit's hand implies that female desire, in the Ming frame, had to be extinguished. The Mouse Spirit (ch. 82, line 5981) calls herself the "adopted daughter" of Li Jing — a projection of the Ming folk custom of adopted daughters, revealing the庇护 (patronage) network behind that custom.

Dress, Diet, Burial, Exam ↔ four rites

Dress: the Monk sewing Wukong a robe and the tight-fillet (ch. 14, line 1393) is the literary projection of the Ming monastic-dress system — the fillet is the extreme form of dress-discipline made visible. Guanyin granting the embroidered kasaya and nine-ringed staff (ch. 12, line 1285) is the royal bestowal of vestments, projecting the throne-led Buddhist affairs of the Ming. The Queen of Women's "dragon robe" (ch. 54) is a deliberate transgression of the rank-coded female dress law.

Diet: the Ginseng Fruit (ch. 24, line 2106) binds eating to longevity — the extreme imagining of Ming Daoist ingestion (fushi) and the food-as-medicine system theorised in Li Shizhen's Compendium of Materia Medica (1578). The Peach Banquet (ch. 5, line 726) is the extreme form of the royal tribute system — the invitation list reveals the power network behind tribute; Wukong's exclusion is power's exclusion. At Chechi the Daoists monopolise the state in the name of sacrifice (ch. 44, line 3443), a miniature of the Jiajing emperors' favourites Shao Yuanjie and Tao Zhongwen.

Burial: Emperor Taizong's tour of the underworld and return (ch. 10, line 1149) projects the rank-coded Ming funeral rite and the "resurrection privilege" of supreme authority. Liu Quan bearing melons to the underworld and his wife's borrowing-a-corpse return (ch. 11, line 1219) projects the Ming ghost-marriage custom and the Zhongyuan (Hungry-Ghost) Festival. Squire Kou's return from death via Wukong's infernal pull (ch. 97, line 6907) projects the folk belief that the underworld can be bargained with.

Exam: the diviner Yuan Shoucheng's guidance (ch. 9, line 1132) projects the Ming custom of divining before the exams; Wei Zheng as chief councillor executing the Dragon (ch. 10, line 1158) projects the examination-and-office path; and the Tang Monk as "scripture pilgrim" is a divine mission substituting for exam merit — the pilgrimage is the theologised examination.
