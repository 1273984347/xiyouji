Zen Koan vs. Inner-Alchemy

ESSAY · SUMMARY TRANSLATION
One monkey, two pairs of glasses — the sudden enlightenment of Chan and the gradual discipline of Neidan.
Source: A summary translation of docs/04-文化与历史背景/西游与禅宗公案专题.md. The original Chinese essay compares two competing readings of the same hundred-chapter novel. This English version preserves the core contrast in condensed form.

One Wukong, Two Pairs of Glasses ↔ sudden vs. gradual

The strangest thing about Journey to the West is not that it tells a scripture-seeking tale, but that the same hundred chapters can be read in two opposite directions. One road leads to Chan (Zen) Buddhism's sudden enlightenment — direct apprehension beyond words. The other leads to Daoist inner-alchemy's (Neidan) gradual cultivation — measured stages and precise timing. The novel itself plants both readings: Wukong's Dharma name comes from Subhuti's line "break stubborn emptiness, you must realize emptiness," where the character kong (空, emptiness) echoes the Chan heartbeat of "dwelling nowhere"; yet the eighty-one tribulations were later dismantled by Qing-dynasty alchemical commentators into a step-by-step cultivation chart. "Wukong" is at once a Chan koan and an alchemical symbol of the body's structure. This essay does not judge which is "truer"; it spreads the tension between them — and asks what that tension reveals about the history of reading.

· · ·

The Chan Reading: The Unspeakable Transmission ↔ koan as method

Chan's four-phrase style — "a separate transmission outside scripture, not founded on words, pointing directly to the mind, seeing one's nature and becoming Buddha" — resonates with the novel more deeply than most readers suppose. First, the name is itself a koan. Subhuti naming the stone monkey "Wukong" is a miniature Chan huatou; "realization" here is not accumulated knowledge but a single turn of self-recognition — matching Huineng's "one thought of confusion, a mortal; one thought of awakening, a Buddha." Second, sudden awakening recurs throughout. Wukong's moments of insight are not gradual inference but a sudden seeing-through of delusion. Third, the golden headband reads as bizuo — the Zen master's fierce prod that drives the student to the cliff-edge of letting go. The pain is not external removal but the mind taming itself, akin to the koan's "bucket-bottom falls out." The essence of this reading is in four characters: the untransmitted wonder — it refuses to reduce the novel to a checklist and insists that what matters most lies beyond words.

· · ·

The Neidan Reading: Timing and the Chart ↔ gradual as map

Qing readers swapped the "untransmitted wonder" for an "order to be followed." The Zhengdao and Zhenquan editions rewrote the whole narrative as an alchemical textbook. Characters become the five-phases body structure: Wukong is Metal Lord (Monkey), Bajie is Wood Mother (Pig), Sha Seng is Yellow Dame (Earth, harmonizing metal and wood), the White Dragon Horse is the Will-Horse. The pilgrimage is no longer a journey but a set of internal "medicines" and "cauldrons"; subduing demons is refining the inner "thief." Plot becomes firing-times: the eighty-one tribulations are the ninety-nine number's cultivation rhythm; the forty-nine days in the Bagua furnace are the gentle-and-forceful fires of alchemy; Vulture Peak is not a geographic endpoint but the inner symbol of completion. Where the Chan reading sees a single sudden turn, the alchemical reading sees an order that cannot skip a single step. Its power is that it gives the chaotic narrative a graspable skeleton — turning a "god-demon novel" into a "cultivation manual."

· · ·

The Tension: Who Gets to Re-read Wukong ↔ canon as attitude

Layering the two glasses, the sharpest contradiction is that the same text yields "sudden" to Chan and "gradual" to Neidan. This is not accidental misreading but the competitive occupation of a narrative by two paradigms. One tension is the textualization of sudden-vs-gradual: Huineng and Shenxiu's debate lives on in the novel, which satisfies both — Wukong "returns in a single thought" yet "becomes true through ordeals." Another is words "established" or "not": Chan flaunts "not founded on words," yet the hundred chapters are plainly words; Neidan does the opposite, literalizing every phrase into oral formula. The deepest tension is who has the right to re-read: in the Ming the novel was popular god-demon fiction; under the Qing, beneath literary inquisition, scholars elevated it into an alchemical secret code — reading what you needed as a refuge. The 1955 People's Literature edition then stripped the alchemical shell and returned to the literary Shide tang base text. Choice of base edition has always been a question of attitude: choose the alchemical commentary and the novel is a scripture of cultivation; choose Shide tang and it is literature. Every "edition choice" is an answer to "what is Journey to the West."

· · ·

Conclusion: Richness Lies in Holding Both ↔ open structure

What makes the novel fascinating is precisely its refusal to be colonized by a single frame. The name "Wukong" is already a Chan sign; the structure "eighty-one tribulations" was read by alchemists as a cultivation chart — Chan and Dan, sudden and gradual, coexist and even complete each other. The koan reading reminds us that what matters is the beyond-words moment of recognition; the alchemical reading reminds us that cultivation has rhythm and order, and the body's refinement is equally serious. Both together say one thing: the novel is never about "fetching a scroll from the West" but about "how to settle this one mind." Qing scholars read it as alchemy, modern scholars read it back as fiction, and future readers may find a third or fourth Journey. The debate over versions and readings has no end, because the book itself permits being rewritten again and again — is that not exactly its greatness?
