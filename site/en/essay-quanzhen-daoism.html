Quanzhen Daoism in the Journey to the West

ESSAY · E10 · CULTURE
A Buddhist plot, a Daoist grammar: how the Quanzhen school's inner alchemy secretly structures the novel.
A curated English summary of the project's Chinese essay şiyóu yǔ Dàojiào Quánzhēn Pài Zhuān tí (A5 · W223). Dates and doctrinal claims are taken from that source; no figures are invented. This is a summary translation, not a full rendering.

Introduction: A Quanzhen Reading ↔ the novel's "cultivation grammar"

The Journey to the West tells a Buddhist story — a monk fetches scriptures from India — but its vocabulary of cultivation is Daoist, and specifically that of the Quanzhen (全真, "Complete Perfection") school. Founded by Wang Chongyang (王重阳, 1113–1170) in 1167, Quanzhen rose to become the dominant state-recognised Daoist order of the Yuan and Ming. Its three pillars are unity of the three teachings (三教合一 — Confucian, Buddhist, Daoist), dual cultivation of nature and life (性命双修), and inner alchemy (内丹学). This essay follows the project's Chinese专题 şiyóu yǔ Dàojiào Quánzhēn Pài Zhuān tí in reading the novel as, in effect, a Quanzhen text in Buddhist costume.

The Inner-Alchemy Codebook ↔ names are terms

The novel's pet names for its heroes are not decorations; they are a lexicon of inner alchemy. The "mind-monkey" xinyuan (心猿) is Wukong — the untamed mind. "Metal Sire" Jingong (金公, lead = the true yang of the kidneys) is also Wukong. "Wood Mother" Mumu (木母, mercury = the true yin of the heart) is Bajie. "Spoon of the Elixir" Daogui (刀圭, a measure of compounded medicine = the harmonising earth of the spleen) is Sha Wujing. Chapter titles such as "the mind-monkey returns to the right" (心猿归正) and "metal and wood share the origin" (金木同元) are Quanzhen formulations lifted straight into the narrative.

Four Core Concepts ↔ mapped onto the plot

  • Dual cultivation of nature and life (性命双修). "Nature" (性) is mind/spirit; "life" (命) is body/energy. Quanzhen cultivates nature first, life second. Wukong's arc is exactly this: the unruly "nature" of the Great Sage → the disciplined "nature" of the Band-Tightening → the completed nature-and-life of the Fighting Buddha.
  • Unity of the three teachings (三教合一). The pilgrimage itself is a three-teaching network: the Buddha initiates, Guanyin executes, the Tang emperor (Confucian state) sponsors, and Daoist gods (Venus, local landlords) protect. Enlightenment is certified by all three.
  • Inner alchemy (内丹学). The eighty-one tribulations are the furnace's staged refinement — "refine essence to vapour, vapour to spirit, spirit to void, void to the Dao" — each trial a checkpoint on the body-as-crucible.
  • Quanzhen precepts (全真戒律). "Complete the perfected nature" — Wukong's journey is an embodied Quanzhen discipline even though he never "joins a monastery."

Conclusion: A "Chinese Classical Quanzhen Text" ↔ why it matters

Read this way, the Journey to the West is less a Buddhist scripture than a Daoist cultivation manual wearing a Buddhist plot. Its "修行语法" (the grammar of spiritual practice) is Quanzhen: the names, the chapter titles, the staged ordeals, and the final "completing the perfected nature" all belong to the Complete Perfection school. That is why a novel about a Buddhist monk could become, for Ming readers, a handbook of inner transformation — and why the project treats its allegorical layer as primary, not decorative.
