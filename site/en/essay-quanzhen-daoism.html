Introduction: A Quanzhen Reading ↔ the novel's "cultivation grammar"

The Journey to the West tells a Buddhist story — a monk fetches scriptures from India — but its vocabulary of cultivation is Daoist, and specifically that of the Quanzhen ("Complete Perfection") school. Founded by Wang Chongyang (1113–1170) in 1167, Quanzhen rose to become the dominant state-recognised Daoist order of the Yuan and Ming. Its three pillars are unity of the three teachings (— Confucian, Buddhist, Daoist), dual cultivation of nature and life, and inner alchemy. This essay follows the project's Chinese essay şiyóu yǔ Dàojiào Quánzhēn Pài Zhuān tí in reading the novel as, in effect, a Quanzhen text in Buddhist costume.

The Inner-Alchemy Codebook ↔ names are terms

The novel's pet names for its heroes are not decorations; they are a lexicon of inner alchemy. The "mind-monkey" xinyuan is Wukong — the untamed mind. "Metal Sire" Jingong (Jingong, lead = the true yang of the kidneys) is also Wukong. "Wood Mother" Mumu (Mumu, mercury = the true yin of the heart) is Bajie. "Spoon of the Elixir" Daogui (Daogui, a measure of compounded medicine = the harmonising earth of the spleen) is Sha Wujing. Chapter titles such as "the mind-monkey returns to the right" and "metal and wood share the origin" are Quanzhen formulations lifted straight into the narrative.

Four Core Concepts ↔ mapped onto the plot

  • Dual cultivation of nature and life. "Nature"(xing) is mind/spirit; "life"(ming) is body/energy. Quanzhen cultivates nature first, life second. Wukong's arc is exactly this: the unruly "nature" of the Great Sage → the disciplined "nature" of the Band-Tightening → the completed nature-and-life of the Fighting Buddha.
  • Unity of the three teachings. The pilgrimage itself is a three-teaching network: the Buddha initiates, Guanyin executes, the Tang emperor (Confucian state) sponsors, and Daoist gods (Venus, local landlords) protect. Enlightenment is certified by all three.
  • Inner alchemy. The eighty-one tribulations are the furnace's staged refinement — "refine essence to vapour, vapour to spirit, spirit to void, void to the Dao" — each trial a checkpoint on the body-as-crucible.
  • Quanzhen precepts. "Complete the perfected nature" — Wukong's journey is an embodied Quanzhen discipline even though he never "joins a monastery."

Conclusion: A "Chinese Classical Quanzhen Text" ↔ why it matters

Read this way, the Journey to the West is less a Buddhist scripture than a Daoist cultivation manual wearing a Buddhist plot. Its "xiuxing yufa" (the grammar of spiritual practice) is Quanzhen: the names, the chapter titles, the staged ordeals, and the final "completing the perfected nature" all belong to the Complete Perfection school. That is why a novel about a Buddhist monk could become, for Ming readers, a handbook of inner transformation — and why the project treats its allegorical layer as primary, not decorative.
